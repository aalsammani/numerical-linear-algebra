About the course#

Scope#

The course is an introduction to numerical linear algebra at the graduate level. It is written for students in applied mathematics, mathematics, statistics, data science, engineering and the computational sciences. The central questions are how to compute the solutions of the standard problems of linear algebra, how much the answers can be trusted when every operation is rounded, and how the algorithms scale to large problems.

The material is organized in six parts. Part I develops the language of the subject: matrix products and their cost, orthogonal transformations, norms, and the singular value decomposition. Part II explains floating-point arithmetic and the two ideas that organize all of error analysis, conditioning (a property of the problem) and stability (a property of the algorithm). Parts III and IV treat linear systems and least-squares problems through the LU, Cholesky and QR factorizations. Part V treats the eigenvalue problem, from the power method to the shifted QR algorithm that underlies every modern eigenvalue solver. Part VI turns to large problems: sparse matrices, Krylov subspace methods such as conjugate gradients and GMRES, preconditioning, and randomized algorithms for low-rank approximation and least squares.

Prerequisites#

Students should be comfortable with undergraduate linear algebra (vector spaces, linear maps, rank, determinants, eigenvalues, the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices) and with basic real analysis at the level of limits and continuity. Programming experience in Python with NumPy is assumed at the level of writing functions and loops and manipulating arrays. No prior course in numerical analysis is required.

How the materials fit together#

Each chapter consists of the notes and a lab notebook, and some chapters add a case study. The notes are self-contained, and every theorem is either proved, proved in outline with the outline labeled as such, or cited to a specific source. The notebooks repeat only as much theory as they need, and they cite the numbered results of the notes. Exercises at the end of each chapter range from conceptual questions to small projects. Solutions to about a third of them are included in the notes; the remaining solutions are in the instructor’s edition.

Two conventions run through the whole course. First, every numerical claim in the notes has been computed, and the lab lets you reproduce it. Second, run times are always presented as observations on a particular machine; the machine-independent measures of cost are flop counts and the amount of data moved.

Suggested schedule#

Covering every section of the notes at a comfortable pace takes about 36 lectures of 75 minutes. A 15-week semester with two lectures per week has about 30, so a semester course omits or shortens some material; the instructor’s edition marks what can be left out of each chapter. Textbooks#

The notes follow no single textbook, but they draw on and cite a small set of standard references. Trefethen and Bau [1997] is the closest in spirit and level. Golub and Van Loan [2013] is the comprehensive reference for algorithms, Higham [2002] for rounding error analysis, and Demmel [1997] for an applied treatment with attention to software. Saad [2003] covers the iterative methods of Part VI in depth.
